01

The problem

My client leases aircraft engines to airline operators. Operators don't pay a flat fee — they pay based on how the engine is actually used: flight distances, environmental conditions, load cycles. At the end of each billing period, the client's team collected flight data and calculated the final amount owed by each operator.

This process was called reconciliation. It lived entirely in a shared Excel spreadsheet. Completing it for all operators took approximately six months every billing cycle.

Reconciliation for all operators took approximately six months every billing cycle — six sheets, dozens of columns, maintained manually by a team of 20–30 specialists. By the time a problem was visible, it was usually too late to fix it cheaply.

The financial stakes were enormous. If the gap between what an operator had contracted to pay and what they actually owed went undetected until the end of the period, the client could find itself owing operators millions of dollars — or operators could face unexpected debt they couldn't repay. Six months was a long time to find out you had a problem.

02

My role

I was the UX Designer on the project, working alongside a UI Design Lead, business stakeholders, domain experts, and front-end and back-end development teams. I owned requirements gathering, wireframing, feedback cycles with business and experts, collaboration with the UI lead on design system consistency, developer handoff, and continued support through implementation and edge cases.

The project ran for approximately 1.5 years. I was there through deployment.

03

Process

Discovery

I started with in-depth interviews with the reconciliation experts — the specialists who had built and maintained the Excel-based process for years.

My goal wasn't to understand the financial calculations themselves. There were dozens of complex formulas involved, and that's deep domain expertise. My focus was on how specialists worked: which data could be pulled automatically versus entered manually, what constituted an invalid input, what error states were likely, and what information was most critical at each stage.

Discovery interviews focused on how specialists worked, not on the calculations themselves. I needed to know which data could be pulled automatically, what counted as an invalid input, and what experts needed at each stage. The maths wasn't my job. The workflow was.

This distinction matters in complex domain projects. A designer who tries to become a domain expert risks losing sight of the user. My job was to translate expert workflow into a reliable, usable interface.

The pivot that reshaped the project

The initial assumption — shared by the business — was that digitising the Excel tool would be sufficient. Once we delivered the first version, it became clear that assumption was wrong.

Each operator contract is highly customised. Specialists needed extensive ability to override, adjust, and annotate calculated values. The application couldn't just display data — it had to support a complex expert-driven editing workflow while keeping a full audit trail of every change.

This pivot extended the project significantly. It also led to the most important design decisions I made.

Key design decisions
Decision 01
Architecture

Step-by-step architecture over a single scrollable view. The original Excel tool was one long continuous sheet. I restructured the application into discrete sequential steps — each representing one calculation phase whose output feeds into the next. In a context where errors carry million-dollar consequences, this wasn't a UX preference — it was risk management.

Decision 02
Edit mode

Read-only mode with explicit edit activation. I introduced a read-only default state for all editable fields. Specialists had to consciously enter edit mode before making changes. The added friction was intentional — accidental edits to data that directly affects payment calculations wasn't a scenario I was willing to design around.

Decision 03
Affordance

Visual differentiation of editable fields. Even in read-only mode, editable inputs are displayed as input fields rather than plain text. This tells specialists at a glance which values they can override and which are system-calculated. The trade-off was slightly reduced scannability — a trade-off I accepted given the importance of that affordance.

Ten sequential steps, visible in the left-hand navigation. Everything defaults to read-only — specialists activate edit mode explicitly before touching anything. Editable fields look like inputs even when locked, so it's always clear what can be changed and what can't.

04

The solution

The delivered portal consisted of two parts: a summary dashboard giving specialists an immediate overview of which operators have overpayments or underpayments, and a detailed ten-tab application showing the full calculation breakdown for each operator — with controlled editing, change tracking, and audit trail.

The summary dashboard gives specialists an immediate overview of which operators have overpayments or underpayments. A process that previously took six months to complete now updates continuously — giving the business time to renegotiate contracts before discrepancies become a crisis.

Eleven tabs, each covering a separate calculation phase — from contract summary to final reconciliation amount. Some tabs contain dozens of screens accounting for edge cases, custom contract variations, and error states. This is what it takes to replace a six-month manual process with something reliable.

05

Outcome

For the roughly 20% of operators with standard contracts, reconciliation data that previously required days of manual work per operator is now generated automatically and updated monthly with no manual data entry.

For the remaining operators with complex customised contracts, the application dramatically reduces manual effort — specialists no longer navigate between multiple systems copying data into Excel. The calculation logic is now reliable, shared, and auditable across the entire team.

Most importantly, the business now has real-time visibility into operator payment status throughout the billing period. Discrepancies that previously went undetected for up to six months can now be identified after a single quarter — giving the client time to renegotiate contracts before they result in multi-million dollar liabilities.

06

What I learned

High financial stakes change how you design. Features that would be reasonable in a lower-stakes context — like editable fields that behave like plain text — become risks when a misclick can affect a million-dollar calculation. Designing for consequence means sometimes adding friction deliberately.

I also learned that a 1.5-year project will always be a different project at the end than it was at the start. The ability to stay useful as scope evolves — without losing sight of the original user need — is something I hadn't fully appreciated before this project.
